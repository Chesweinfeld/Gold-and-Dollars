The holdings map shows where reserves sit. This one shows the lines between countries: who lends the United States its money, and whose currency the rest of the world owes its debts in.

Who holds US Treasuries

An asset for the holder — and a liability of the United States

Every arc is a real country pair: a foreign holder at one end, the United States at the other. Arc width is the value held. This is the only genuinely bilateral reserve-asset series anyone publishes — and it is a record of custody, not ownership, which is why Belgium and the Cayman Islands sit so high.

Whose currency the world owes in

Liabilities — what countries owe, net

The international banking system's net position with each country — what it is owed, minus what it owes — split by the currency the debt is written in. This is what makes a currency a reserve currency: if your obligations are in dollars, you must hold dollars. The arrow points at whoever owes; width is the size of the net position.

Two things the arrows do not say. The colour means denominated in, never lent by — the BIS publishes no lender-country by borrower-country matrix, so the arc begins at a currency's central bank rather than at a creditor. And this is a net position against the banking system as a whole, not a bilateral pair: gross claims and gross liabilities are both in the tooltip, because netting hides how large a balance sheet the number came out of.

What each of these arcs is actually claiming

Two flow maps, two different kinds of line, and it matters which is which.

The Treasury arcs are real country pairs — of custodians

TIC records who the US-based custodian says holds the security. Treasury's own footnote says the table "may not provide a precise accounting of individual country ownership". Euroclear sits in Belgium, so Belgium's $477bn is mostly other people's; Ireland, Luxembourg and the Caymans are fund domiciles. A Saudi holding managed through London appears as the United Kingdom. Read the top of this list as a map of the custody industry as much as of national wealth.

The series also mixes official and private holders. Only the world total is split, and it is currently 42% foreign official — so a country's line is not its central bank's reserves.

The currency arcs are not country pairs at all

The BIS publishes cross-border positions by counterparty country and by currency of denomination, but only for all reporting banks combined. There is no published matrix of which country lent to which. An arrow into Warsaw coloured for the euro means Poland owes that much in euro on net; it does not mean Germany lent it. Drawing it as though it did would be inventing the most interesting part of the picture.

Net is a direction, and it hides a balance sheet

Each arrow is claims minus liabilities, so it answers "which way does the obligation run" and nothing else. A country with $2tn of claims against $1.9tn of liabilities and one with $150bn against $50bn both show a $100bn arrow. The gross pair is in the tooltip and the table for that reason.

Netting also cuts financial centres down to something readable. London carries $7.2tn of gross claims because it intermediates; on net the position is a small fraction of that. Gross would put the United Kingdom second in the world and mean almost nothing by it.

Coverage is not constant

Until 2016 Treasury published "Caribbean Banking Centers" and "Oil Exporters" as groups instead of their members, so the early months place less of the total on named countries — 71% at the low point against 96% today. The figure under the map is the share currently drawable, and it moves as you scrub back.
