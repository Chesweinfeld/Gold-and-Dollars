The Reserve Currency Map

Every central bank holds a pile of someone else's money. This is where those piles sit, how they moved east over sixty-five years, and which currencies they are denominated in — from the two institutions that actually measure it.

Where the reserves are held

Assets — what countries hold

One circle per country, area proportional to the reserves it holds. The orange track is the centre of gravity of the world's reserves — every holder treated as a point mass at its own centroid, averaged on the sphere. It starts in the mid-Atlantic and ends in Central Asia.

The map looks straight down on the North Pole. Every reserve currency but the Australian dollar is issued north of the tropics, as is every one of the twenty largest holders, so the pole is the middle of this subject rather than its edge — and the track's drift east becomes what it physically is, a rotation. Seen from above the pole the Earth turns anticlockwise, so east runs anticlockwise here too: the Americas are on the right, Asia on the left, and the track sweeps leftwards as it goes east. Zoom to Europe for the dozen holders that overlap at this scale.

2025 is still filling in — of 182 reporters have filed. The circles are real; the track for this year is not comparable with the complete ones behind it.

What these two maps cannot be joined into

The obvious next question — which countries hold dollars, and which hold euros — has no published answer, and the gap is deliberate. The IMF collects COFER on the explicit condition that individual countries are never identified, because a handful of holders are large enough that a country breakdown would effectively publish their reserve managers' positions. So the first chart is who holds, never in what, and the second is in what, never who.

Anyone drawing arrows from issuers to holders on a map like this is inventing them. There are no arrows here for that reason.

There are two flows that can honestly be drawn, and they have a page of their own: who holds US Treasuries — a real country pair on both ends, monthly since 2000 — and which currency the world's cross-border debts are written in.

Three more things worth knowing before reading too much into it

  • Gold is valued at market. A country's gold line moves with the gold price even when it has not touched an ounce. The gold measure here is a value, never a quantity.
  • The euro band before 1999 is its predecessors — the Deutsche mark, French franc, Netherlands guilder and European currency unit, which COFER identified separately until they were replaced. Stacking them into the euro band shows the continuity; it is a construction, and the seam is marked on the chart.
  • Shares are of allocated reserves. Before 2000 a large slice of world reserves was unallocated — held by countries that did not report a currency breakdown — so early shares describe only the reporting subset. In December 2025 the IMF removed the unallocated portion entirely and revised back to 2000Q1, which is why the series now accounts for the whole.

Both maps use a Lambert azimuthal equal-area projection centred on the North Pole. The centre of the disc is the pole, the outer circle is the South Pole, and every country covers its true share of the map — no landmass can masquerade as importance, which is also why the holdings map draws circles rather than colouring countries in. Shape is what pays for that: it distorts further out, and Australia and New Zealand, at about 90% of the radius, are smeared around the rim. Antarctica is not drawn.

The zoom is a camera, not a filter. It enlarges the globe and frames a window on it, leaving every circle, arc width and label the size it was — so a European circle stays comparable with the Chinese one now outside the frame, and the figures beneath the map go on describing the world. Europe is framed on 12°E, which puts it at the bottom of the enlarged disc, where north comes out up and east comes out right.

Every figure on this page is rebuilt from those sources by the scripts in src/reserves/, each of which prints its own validation report and refuses to write output that fails. The country totals and the IMF world total are compiled independently and agree to within 6% in every year they overlap.
